Theorem 1: Infinitely many primes (Euclid)

Statement: Infinitely many primes (Euclid)

Hypotheses: None

Conclusion: there are infinitely many primes

Definitions: prime, infinitely many

Proof sketch:

Step 1: Assume finitely many primes p_1,...,p_n

Step 2: Define N = p_1 * p_2 * ... * p_n + 1

Step 3: N > 1, so N has a prime factor p

Step 4: If p = p_i for some i, then p divides N and p_i divides N

Step 5: But p_i cannot divide N - p_1*...*p_n = 1, contradiction

Step 6: So p is a new prime not in the list

Theorem 1: Euclid: there are infinitely many primes

Statement: Euclid: there are infinitely many primes

Hypotheses: none

Conclusion: infinitely many primes exist

Definitions: Euclid, prime, infinite

Proof:

Step 1: Assume finitely many primes $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$.

Step 2: Let $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n + 1$.

Step 3: $N$ has a prime factor $q$. Since $p_i \mid (N-1)$ but $p_i \nmid N$, we get $q \neq p_i$ for all $i$.

Step 4: Contradiction: $q$ is a prime not in our list.

Theorem 2: Fermat's Little: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ for prime $p$, $\gcd

Statement: Fermat's Little: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ for prime $p$, $\gcd

Hypotheses: p prime, gcd(a,p)=1

Conclusion: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$

Definitions: Fermat, modular arithmetic, prime

Proof:

Step 1: Consider $\{a, 2a, \ldots, (p-1)a\}$ mod $p$. These are all distinct (if $ia \equiv ja$, then $p \mid a(i-j)$, impossible since $\gcd(a,p)=1$ and $0 < |i-j| < p$).

Step 2: So the set is a permutation of $\{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}$.

Step 3: Product: $a^{p-1} \cdot (p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod{p}$.

Step 4: Cancel $(p-1)!$: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$.

Theorem 3: Wilson: $

Statement: Wilson: $

Hypotheses: p > 1 integer

Conclusion: $(p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod{p} \Leftrightarrow p$ prime

Definitions: Wilson, factorial, modular

Proof:

Step 1: Forward ($\Rightarrow$): for each $a \in \{1,\ldots,p-1\}$, pair $a$ with $a^{-1}$. Only $a=1$ and $a=p-1$ are self-inverse. Product of remaining $(p-3)/2$ pairs gives 1. Total: $1 \cdot (p-1) \cdot 1 = p-1 \equiv -1$.

Step 2: Backward ($\Leftarrow$): if $p = ab$ with $1 < a,b < p$, then $a \mid (p-1)!$ but $a \nmid -1$. Contradiction.
